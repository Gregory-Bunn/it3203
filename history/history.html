The Foundations (1969–1990)

The creation of the internet is attributed to the United States Department of Defense’s Advanced Research Projects Agency, DARPA. This nascent form of the internet, referred to as ARPANET and introduced foundational concepts such as packet switching, creating the first network between separate computers in 1969.

The first web browser wouldn't come until over two decades later where, in 1990, English computer scientist Tim Berners-Lee at CERN developed a program called "WorldWideWeb." WorldWideWeb was part of a larger effort by Berners-Lee to create a system to better handle large amounts of data and facilitate collaboration within and outside CERN. His efforts also incorporated earlier concepts such as hypertext as a way to connect documents to each other. These advances led to the creation of the first web server, the HTTP protocol, URLs, and other foundational technologies still in use today.

After CERN made the code available to for public use in 1993, the internet would begin to grow explosively. The National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA) would release the first browser made for the general public, Mosaic, that year. Mosaic was the first web browser with the ability to display images within the same document as text, rather than necessitating a separate window. With the new general availability of the web browser, the number of websites grew explosively.

The Browser Wars (1994–2008)

In 1994, Netscape Navigator would be released, quickly gaining the majority of market share among web browsers. It changed the landscape of what a web browser could be, introducing Java and JavaScript. These two technologies allowed Netscape Navigator to display novel features such as dynamically updating pages, animation, and interactivity.

That same year, Tim Berners-Lee would found the World Wide Web Consortium, the W3C. Its primary purpose is to develop open standards and guidelines for the web, ensuring that websites remain compatible across different browsers and devices. The consortium originally consisted of just two sponsoring organizations, the European Commission and DARPA, but has since grown into a global organization with 329 members with 18 offices around the world.

Microsoft would then join the race in 1995, developing Internet Explorer. Initially, it was very barebones, lacking features that Netscape supported, but would eventually become more technically competitive with version 2.0, mere months later. 2.0 would be bundled with the “Microsoft Plus!” addon for Windows 95, and would soon become the most popular browser for the next 13 years.

The Modern Era & Shifting Dominance (2004–Present)

Another notable release would come in 2004, with the release of Mozilla Firefox. Firefox is often seen as a spiritual successor to Netscape Navigator as Mozilla was founded by developers hailing from Netscape. [7] Firefox would be the first to seriously challenge Internet Explorer’s market dominance due to its higher performance and security, amassing over 60 million downloads in its first nine months of release. It would peak in 2009 with a share of 31.82%. [8] This number was larger than some versions of Internet Explorer, but Firefox never grew larger than Internet Explorer as a whole.

Internet Explorer’s dominance would soon begin waning in 2008, with Google Chrome having released in September. Google would take a similar approach as Microsoft did to market and distribute their software. As Microsoft would distribute Internet Explorer in the most popular operating system, Google would market their browser in the most popular search engine. This, along with enhanced speed and a new ecosystem incorporating the Chrome Web Store to install add-ons allowed Chrome to quickly grow to dominance, eventually overtaking both Firefox and Internet Explorer, reaching a peak of 72% in 2025.

Initially, web browsers were restricted to desktop systems due to the non-existence of other devices, as well as existing devices not having the capability to make use of a web browser. Today, web browsers can be found on a great deal of devices, such as smartphones, laptops, tablets, and even smart televisions and game systems. On many of these platforms, Google Chrome remains the dominant web browser.
